# Maison Lumière: A Data-Driven Growth Diagnostic

*A case study in applying management-consulting analytical frameworks to a growing luxury fashion brand's own transactional data.*

## Problem

Maison Lumière, a luxury fashion house operating six retail stores across Europe and North America, three wholesale partnerships (Net-a-Porter, Mytheresa, Browns), and a direct-to-consumer ecommerce channel, had grown revenue to approximately €10.8M annually — but leadership lacked a single, trustworthy view of *why*. Finance, marketing, and retail operations each reported from disconnected spreadsheets and platform dashboards. No one could answer, with real numbers, three questions that determine whether this growth is durable: Which channel actually produces valuable customers? Which levers move the business the most? And where is real risk concentrating beneath a healthy top line?

## Approach

Rather than layer another dashboard on top of existing reports, the engagement built a single analytical platform directly on the brand's real transactional data — retail POS, ecommerce events, influencer campaign records, wholesale orders, supplier records, and cost-center actuals — and applied a standard toolkit of management-consulting and corporate-finance frameworks directly to it, growing into a 20-calculator decision-intelligence suite: customer lifetime value, acquisition cost, and churn analysis (including a predictive risk-scoring model); difference-in-differences campaign measurement; Monte Carlo revenue simulation and trend/pattern decomposition; sensitivity (tornado) analysis and a McKinsey-style value driver tree; price/volume/mix revenue-growth decomposition; a customer-cohort Growth Bridge (expansion, new business, churn, price — the standard consulting revenue bridge); CAGR and Rule of 72; and a full corporate-finance stack — discounted cash flow valuation, CAPM, WACC, and inventory economics (EOQ, now including a real reorder point sourced from actual supplier lead time) — with CAPM, WACC, and the DCF wired together so a single change in market assumptions flows through to firm value automatically. A later pass added the SAP CO/FI-equivalent layer this diagnostic draws on below: a Consolidated P&L walking real revenue down to EBITDA and net margin with auto-generated management commentary, a Supplier Intelligence scorecard with automatic risk alerts, and a Cost Allocation Engine that applies activity-based costing to reveal true profitability by channel instead of a naive revenue-only split. The platform is rounded out by a Benchmark Intelligence page holding the brand's own metrics against real, cited luxury-industry benchmark reports, and a Consulting Summary that compiles the platform's own findings into a structured deliverable live.

Two disciplines governed every model: **first, use only real computed data** — no figure appears in the platform that was not derived from an actual query against the brand's own records; and **second, name every assumption explicitly** where real data ran out (e.g., an EBITDA-margin-based DCF, a documented industry benchmark for luxury-goods price elasticity, external population reference data for market sizing). The result is a diagnostic that a CFO can trust precisely because it does not hide where the real data ends and a modeling assumption begins — a discipline now formalized platform-wide in `DATA_AUDIT.md`, which labels every metric on every page as real, derived-real, illustrative, or an external benchmark. Every figure in this case study is drawn from a page built to that same standard, presented in the platform's Document-style report layout: one headline finding, three key numbers, and the underlying table, on every page.

## Key Findings

**1. Customer acquisition is a single point of failure.** Every one of the brand's 97 tracked direct customers converted through an influencer touchpoint — zero converted organically. The influencer channel itself is healthy (a 4.31:1 CLV-to-CAC ratio, comfortably above the 3:1 profitability threshold), but the brand has no second acquisition engine. Meanwhile, €403,834 in tracked "marketing" cost-center spend over the same period produced no directly traceable acquisitions at all — a real attribution gap, not a performance failure per se, but one that hides whether that spend is working.

**2. Retention, not acquisition, is the more urgent problem.** 85 of 97 real customers (87.6%) had not purchased in over 90 days as of the most recent data. Segment-level analysis shows this is not confined to one-time buyers: even the "returning" and "VIP" segments show high inactivity. A customer-cohort Growth Bridge confirms this from an independent angle: splitting the purchase history into two real cohort periods, zero customers bought in both — every euro of period-over-period revenue change is new-customer revenue against churned-customer revenue, with no expansion or price effect achievable until repeat-purchase behavior exists at all. The brand is acquiring customers effectively through one channel and then losing essentially all of them before a second purchase.

**3. Campaign performance is highly dispersed, and spend is not yet following it.** Across 40 historical influencer campaigns, ROI ranges from -88.3% to 334.4% against a platform average of 99.2%; 23 of 40 campaigns sit below that average. The five best-performing creators alone (ROI 140%–334%) demonstrate the channel's ceiling — the gap between average and best-in-class spend allocation is a real, currently uncaptured opportunity.

**4. Revenue is structurally dependent on three wholesale partners.** Wholesale contributes 67.8% of total revenue from just three accounts. A tornado/sensitivity analysis across every major lever in the business (wholesale partner count, store count, average order value, conversion rate, influencer spend) confirms wholesale partner count as by far the dominant driver of total revenue — a ±20% change there moves revenue by ±13.6%, roughly 25 times the impact of an equivalent swing in online conversion rate or AOV. This is the business's single largest concentration risk and its single largest growth lever, simultaneously.

**5. At least one retail location is underperforming and trending the wrong way.** The Paris flagship — one of the brand's largest stores — shows real, uninterrupted revenue decline across three consecutive months (March–May), a 27.7% drop, while company-wide revenue over the same window was mixed but not uniformly declining. This is a store-specific issue, not a market-wide one.

**6. Underlying growth is real but far more volatile than it appears.** A trend/pattern decomposition of monthly revenue finds a genuine 11.3%/month underlying growth trend, but the trend line explains only 36% of month-to-month variation (R² = 0.36) — meaning more than half of the swings investors would see quarter to quarter reflect one-off events (a real Q4 wholesale reorder ramp), not a repeatable seasonal pattern. Extrapolating recent months naively would overstate sustainable growth by an order of magnitude; a disciplined forecast caps the assumption accordingly.

**7. Against real luxury-industry benchmarks, the brand trails on three of five core metrics and leads on two.** Sourced from public luxury-sector reports: gross margin is 55.1% against a 62% industry average and 75% at top-performing houses; influencer ROI is 1.56x against a 2x average; and customer retention (12.4%) sits well below even the low end of the industry's own 25–35% repeat-purchase target — directly corroborating Finding 2. But the brand is genuinely ahead on two metrics: its CLV:CAC ratio (4.31:1) clears the 3:1 minimum-healthy threshold with room to the 5:1 "excellent" mark, and its return rate (10.3%) is well under the 25–40% apparel-ecommerce norm, a real, defensible strength worth protecting rather than a data artifact.

**8. Gross margin looks identical across channels — true margin doesn't, and the gap is almost entirely Retail's real logistics burden.** All three channels report the same ~55.1% gross margin this period, because COGS is applied at the same blended ratio company-wide — gross margin alone can't distinguish channel profitability at all. Allocating the real Logistics cost pool by actual transaction/order count (Retail: 6,201 real transactions; Wholesale: 27 real orders; Ecommerce: 3 real purchases this period) tells a different story: Retail absorbs 99.5% of the €22,321 Logistics pool against just 10.6% of revenue, compressing its true margin to 44.2% — a 10.9-point drop. Wholesale, despite generating 89% of revenue, carries a proportionally lighter logistics load and only compresses to 53.0% (−2.1 points). This is "true profitability by channel" — the number most fashion brands never compute because they default to a revenue-only overhead split, which would have hidden this gap entirely.

**9. Three of nine tracked suppliers fail real delivery-performance thresholds, concentrated in knitwear and outerwear.** Alpine Wool Mill (knitwear) trips both risk flags at once — a 67-day lead time and a 79.4% on-time delivery rate, the weakest of any supplier tracked. Porto Outerwear Manufacturing (83.7% on-time delivery) and Loire Leather Ateliers (a 72-day lead time) each trip one. Knitwear is sourced from only two suppliers total, so Alpine Wool Mill's real risk profile isn't a diversifiable one-off — it's exposure to a thin supplier base in a single category.

## Recommendations

1. **Fund a second, measurable acquisition channel.** Redirect a portion of the untraceable €403,834 marketing spend into either better attribution tooling or directly into the influencer channel, which has already proven a 4.31:1 return — don't continue funding a channel the platform cannot prove is working.
2. **Launch a win-back program targeting the 85 at-risk customers**, prioritized by the segment-level lists this platform now produces automatically, before they age further past the 90-day threshold.
3. **Reallocate gifting budget away from the 23 below-average campaigns toward the five proven top performers**, using the real revenue-per-euro-gifted ratio the platform already computes for every campaign.
4. **Treat wholesale partner concentration as a board-level risk item**, not just a growth story — model a fourth or fifth partner relationship explicitly, since the sensitivity analysis shows no other lever comes close to this one's impact.
5. **Open a Paris-specific operating review** — the three-month decline is real, isolated, and now flagged automatically rather than requiring someone to notice it in a spreadsheet.
6. **Adopt trend-and-pattern decomposition, not naive extrapolation, for board-level forecasts** — present the true 11.3% underlying trend alongside its confidence range, not a headline number inflated by a one-off Q4 event.
7. **Close the gross-margin and retention gaps first; protect the return-rate and CLV:CAC strengths while doing it.** The benchmark comparison ranks where the brand should focus (margin and retention are the largest real gaps to the industry) versus where it's already ahead (returns, unit economics) — a clearer starting priority list than treating every metric as equally urgent.
8. **Reassess channel investment on true margin, not gross margin.** Wholesale's overhead-adjusted profitability (53.0%) holds up better than Retail's (44.2%) — this reinforces, rather than offsets, Finding 4's wholesale-concentration risk: the channel the brand depends on most is also the one converting to profit most efficiently once real costs are allocated. Retail's real logistics burden is the more urgent efficiency question to open, not a reason to deprioritize wholesale diversification.
9. **Open a supplier risk review for Alpine Wool Mill first, then Porto Outerwear and Loire Leather**, before the next reorder cycle — and evaluate a second knitwear supplier given the category currently has only two.

## Quantified Impact

- **Churn win-back (illustrative):** recovering even 10% of the 85 at-risk customers, at their real blended segment CLV (~€7,000–€8,000), represents roughly **€60,000–€68,000** in recoverable customer value — from a single, already-available list, at zero incremental acquisition cost.
- **Campaign reallocation (illustrative):** shifting spend from the worst-performing campaign (-88.3% ROI) toward the proven top-five (140%–334% ROI) turns a loss-making allocation into a materially profitable one on the same budget, using ratios the platform already measures.
- **Concentration risk quantified, not just flagged:** the sensitivity model puts a precise number on what was previously a qualitative worry — a ±20% swing in wholesale partner count is a **±€1.46M** swing in total revenue, 25× the impact of the next-largest lever, turning "we depend on wholesale" into a board-actionable, ranked priority list.
- **Benchmark gap sized, not just flagged:** closing even half the gap between the brand's real 55.1% gross margin and the 62% industry average (3.45 percentage points) would be worth roughly **€372,000** at the current €10.8M revenue base — a concrete target for the margin-improvement work Recommendation 7 prioritizes, not an abstract "get better at margin" instruction.
- **Decision infrastructure, not just insight:** every finding above is now reproducible on demand, not a one-time analysis — the same automated rules that surfaced the Paris decline and the marketing attribution gap will catch the next one.
- **True-margin gap quantified, not just flagged:** Retail's true margin compresses 10.9 points once its real logistics burden is allocated (55.1% gross → 44.2% true), more than 4× the compression Wholesale sees (55.1% → 53.0%, −2.1 points) — despite Wholesale generating 8.4× Retail's revenue. A revenue-only allocation, the industry default, would have hidden this entirely.
- **Supplier risk quantified, not just flagged:** 3 of 9 tracked suppliers (33%) sit outside real delivery-performance thresholds today, actionable before the next purchase order rather than discovered after a stockout — and the EOQ reorder-point math now runs on each supplier's actual lead time and on-time delivery rate instead of a fixed cadence assumption.
